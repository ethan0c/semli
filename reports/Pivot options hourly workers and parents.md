# Test the shift wedge before pivoting anywhere

Neither pivot shows enough paying demand to justify rewriting Semli, and the family-logistics option should be dropped now. The hourly-worker pain is large and well documented: about 60% of service-sector workers get under two weeks' notice of their schedules, and a majority have shift times changed at the last minute. But the people in that market pay for money products, not calendars. The direct comparables charge $10 to $35 a year, and the one breakout worker-side schedule app, NurseGrid, sold for about $25M while unprofitable. The family pain is also real, and parents do pay ($39 to $79 a year per household). But "forward the school email, snap the flyer" is already a feature of Skylight, Cozi Max, Amazon Alexa+, Apple, Gemini and at least eight funded AI startups, and three family-assistant products shut down in 2025–2026. A shared family calendar also breaks when one parent carries an Android phone. Staying in college is not safe either, because syllabus import is commoditized. The one overlap worth money is the student who works hourly shifts: that person sits inside both the college market and the hourly market, and Semli already ships nearly everything they need, including a schedule diff that no competitor was found offering. My recommendation is to keep the codebase, reposition toward "students who work" (with nursing students as the sharpest sub-segment), and spend the next six to eight weeks on a near-zero-code test that also measures pull from non-student hourly workers. Widen to all hourly workers only if that test clears the go criteria below. One caution applies to everything here: nearly all figures come from search snippets, vendor blogs and competitor-written comparisons, because the App Store, Google Play, Reddit and BLS pages were blocked during research. The uniqueness claims rest on absence of evidence within a limited search, not proof of absence.

## How much weight these findings can carry

Treat this report as a map, not a measurement. **No App Store or Play listing, Reddit thread, or BLS page was read in full.** Ratings, prices and install counts come from search-result summaries or third-party trackers (AppGoblin, AppFollow, JustUseApp) of uncertain date. Many sources are written by competitors with an interest: PicCal writes the Starbucks and WorkJam guides, getsense.ai writes the "Skylight hidden costs" and "Milo is shutting down" posts, and RemindHer reviews Ohai. Several figures conflict. Skylight's revenue estimates range from $10M to $75M a year, and Ohai's funding is $6M+ in press releases but $44.45M on CB Insights. Neither research stream found first-hand testimony from hourly workers or parents on Reddit, so "what users say" rests mainly on press, academic surveys and one Boston Globe piece. The strongest evidence is academic and government data on the pain: the Shift Project's large employer-linked surveys, BLS counts and a 3,000-parent study in the *Journal of Marriage and Family*. The weakest evidence is exactly what a pivot decision needs most: conversion, retention, CAC and willingness to pay for each segment. **None of the three options has a single verified paid-conversion figure.** That gap is the main reason this report recommends a test rather than a pivot.

| Claim type | Evidence quality | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Hourly schedule pain exists and is widespread | Strong | Shift Project surveys of tens of thousands of workers; BLS; peer-reviewed turnover and sleep studies |
| Parent mental load and app overload | Moderate to strong | JMF study (n≈3,000); Aspen Project Play; vendor stats flagged |
| Competitor features and prices | Moderate | Many independent listings agree, but read via snippets |
| "Nobody does schedule diffing" | Weak to moderate | Absence of evidence in two separate searches |
| Willingness to pay for each pivot | Weak | Price anchors only; no conversion data anywhere |
| Android share of hourly workers and mixed-OS households | Weak | No segment survey found |

## Hourly workers hurt the most and pay the least

The pain case for option (a) is the best-evidenced of anything in the notes. **80.3 million US workers were paid hourly in 2024, 55.6% of wage and salary workers** ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/opub/reports/minimum-wage/2024)). About **8.5 to 8.8 million people held multiple jobs** in 2025–2026 ([BLS table 36](https://www.bls.gov/cps/cpsaat36.htm); [USAFacts](https://usafacts.org/answers/how-many-people-have-multiple-jobs/country/united-states/)). Among service workers at large retail and food chains, **60% get less than two weeks' notice, 57% had last-minute shift-time changes, and 13% had a shift cancelled** ([Shift Project](https://shift.hks.harvard.edu/secure-scheduling/), from a search summary). In New Jersey, 67% of workers whose shift moved got less than 24 hours' warning ([Shift NJ](https://shift.hks.harvard.edu/working-in-the-service-sector-in-new-jersey/)). The consequences are measurable: instability predicts turnover ([ILR Review via Shift](https://shift.hks.harvard.edu/uncertain-time-precarious-schedules-and-job-turnover-in-the-u-s-service-sector)) and worse sleep ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9366729)). Young children of retail and food workers on last-minute schedules average **15 days a year without childcare** ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2023/06/12/unpredictable-work-schedules-for-parents-make-child-care-a-huge-challenge)). Fair Workweek laws exist in Oregon and about ten cities, yet even in Seattle almost half of covered workers still get under two weeks' notice ([Shift fact sheet, Jul 2026](https://shift.hks.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/fww_factsheet_report_final.pdf)), and about ten states preempt local laws ([Clockspot](https://www.clockspot.com/research/predictive-scheduling-laws-by-state)). The broad economy-wide irregular-schedule share is far smaller, about 16% of hourly workers in old EPI data ([EPI](https://www.epi.org/publication/irregular-work-scheduling-and-its-consequences/)). So the real market is service-sector hourly workers at large chains, not "all hourly workers." That is still tens of millions of people.

The pain is concentrated where employer tools fail. SMB schedulers already give workers one-way iCal feeds: 7shifts ([KB](https://kb.7shifts.com/hc/en-us/articles/4861793532691-Sync-Your-7shifts-Schedule-with-Google-and-Apple-Calendar)), Deputy ([help](https://help.deputy.com/hc/en-au/articles/4688796818703-Syncing-your-Deputy-schedule-with-your-own-calendar-application)) and When I Work ([help](https://help.wheniwork.com/articles/syncing-your-schedule-to-a-calendar-app-computer/)). Those feeds lag 2 to 24 hours, need desktop setup, and cover one employer each. The screenshot case is strongest at big chains with closed apps. No official calendar export was found for Starbucks Partner Hours ([PicCal guide](https://piccal.ai/guides/add-starbucks-schedule-to-calendar/), a competitor source) or Me@Walmart ([HR Executive](https://hrexecutive.com/walmarts-new-workforce-app-provides-a-look-into-the-future/)). Target workers install browser extensions to get myTime shifts out ([Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/mytime-to-calendar/odekbnpmjmbggpaeglaljcgapeoknhcp)). **No product found aggregates one worker's shifts across different employers**, and none diffs a revised schedule against saved shifts. Semli's cross-job conflict view and diff land in that gap.

Willingness to pay is where option (a) breaks down. Hourly workers demonstrably pay for money: employer-linked earned-wage-access users paid **about $69 a year in fees** on average ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/research-reports/data-spotlight-developments-in-the-paycheck-advance-market/)), and gig drivers pay $6 to $15 a month for earnings tools like Gridwise ([Gridwise](https://gridwise.io/announcements/details-on-new-plus-and-basic-plans/)). Calendar utilities sit an order of magnitude lower. PicCal's batch scanning, the feature a weekly schedule needs, costs **$1.99 a month or $19.99 a year** ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/piccal-picture-calendar/id6745625648)). Supershift Pro is quoted at $9.99 a year or $39.99 lifetime ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/supershift-shift-work-calendar/id1104165041)), and Nursly at $34.99 a year ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id6758769900)). The category is crowded at the bottom: at least six generic shift calendars show 1M+ Android installs and 4.1 to 4.8 stars ([Google Play: Supershift](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=app.supershift&hl=en_US)), and a 2025–2026 wave of AI screenshot importers (Shift to Calendar, SnapShift, ShiftKal) has negligible traction so far ([AppGoblin](https://appgoblin.info/apps/6755889602)). The history is worse. **NurseGrid reached 260K MAU and was still unprofitable when HealthStream bought it for about $25M** ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2020/healthstream-pays-25m-acquire-portland-startup-nursegrid-maker-mobile-app-nurses/)). Six years later it has about 670K MAU but earns only about $40K a month in education revenue ([Q3 2025 transcript](https://www.aol.com/finance/healthstream-hstm-q3-2025-earnings-231916465.html)). Branch began as a shift-swap app and became a payments company ([American Banker](https://www.americanbanker.com/payments/news/branch-expands-payroll-advance-app-to-all-hourly-workers)), and Shyft sold to employers ([Retail Dive](https://www.retaildive.com/news/shyft-raises-65m-to-fuel-workforce-management-app/542428/)). **No standalone consumer schedule app was found to raise notable venture money in 2020–2026.** Worker-side schedule audiences get monetized through employers, wage access or recruiting, not subscriptions.

Platform risk compounds the pricing problem. No survey of hourly workers' iOS/Android split was found. But Walmart, the largest private employer, handed **740,000 associates Samsung phones** ([Walmart, 2021](https://corporate.walmart.com/news/2021/06/03/walmart-unveils-all-in-one-associate-app-me-walmart-and-gives-740-000-associates-a-new-samsung-smartphone)), and the 1M-install shift apps are Android-heavy. The US overall is roughly 58% iOS and 42% Android ([Digital Silk](https://www.digitalsilk.com/digital-trends/iphone-vs-android-user-stats/)), and lower-wage workers likely skew more Android than that. That is an inference, but a safe one. An iPhone-only hourly-worker app gives up a large share of the market before launch, and a screenshot importer also fails on company-managed phones that block screenshots ([PicCal](https://piccal.ai/use-cases/shift-schedules/)).

## Parents pay, but the feature is already free and the field is crowded

Option (b) has the opposite profile: proven payment, little room. The pain is well documented. **83% of mothers say keeping the family calendar is "mostly me"** ([Journal of Marriage and Family, 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jomf.13057)). Sports parents spend about 3 hours 23 minutes on each practice or game day ([Project Play](https://projectplay.org/news/project-play-survey-parents-spend-3-hours-on-sports-every-day-their-child-plays)) and about **$1,500 a year per child** on youth sports ([Project Play, 2025](https://projectplay.org/news/2025/2/24/project-play-survey-family-spending-on-youth-sports-rises-46-over-five-years)). The Boston Globe ran "Two kids, 12 apps, and zero clue?" this month ([Boston Globe, Oct 5 2026](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/10/05/lifestyle/parents-schools-edtech-apps-platforms-passwords/)). Parents demonstrably pay for this job: **Skylight Calendar Plus costs $79 a year** and gates AI email-, PDF- and flyer-to-calendar import ([Skylight support](https://skylight.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/35984779668379-What-does-a-Skylight-Calendar-cost)). Cozi Gold is $39 a year and Cozi Max, with AI import, is $79 ([Cozi](https://www.cozi.com/cozi-gold/)). Nori Plus is $59.90 a year ([Nori](https://heynori.com/membership)). Co-parents pay **$77 to $353 per parent per year** for TalkingParents or OurFamilyWizard ([divorce.law](https://divorce.law/guides/co-parenting-apps-tools/california/)). On price ceilings alone, parents beat both other segments by two to four times.

The problem is that Semli's core skill is the commodity here. AI import of school emails and flyers ships in **Skylight, Hearth, Cozi Max, Amazon Alexa+, Apple Visual Intelligence, Gemini, and startups Ohai, Nori, Sense, Fambot, Carly, Honeydew, Nuet and Jam**. Amazon adds dates "automatically" when you forward an email to alexa@alexa.com ([About Amazon](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/devices/new-alexa-plus-amazon-devices)). An iPhone 15 Pro or later creates an event from a pointed camera ([Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/phones/iphones/how-to-use-visual-intelligence-on-iphone-to-make-calendar-events)). The leader is formidable. Skylight is bootstrapped, claims 9.3 million users and 99% year-on-year revenue growth, and took a $50M loan in April 2025 ([SG Credit Partners](https://www.sgcreditpartners.com/news/skylight-fuels-family-first-innovation-with-50-million-of-financing-from-sg-credit-partners-and-wingspire-capital/)). It sells hardware at Best Buy and Sam's Club, then converts buyers to the subscription. Funded newcomers keep arriving: **Fambot raised $3.5M and launched Sept 1, 2026 on iOS, Android and web** ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/01/fambot-introduces-an-ai-chief-of-staff-for-families/)). The graveyard is just as visible. **Milo shut down in January 2026**, with its founder saying the technology was "too early to be reliable" ([Alpha Mothers](https://alphamothers.com/resources/milo-ai-app-shut-down-best-alternative-families), a competitor source). **Maple closes Dec 31, 2026** ([Sense blog](https://getsense.ai/blog/posts/maple-is-shutting-down-alternative-2026), also a competitor), and Panasonic's Yohana wound down in September 2025 ([Yohana](https://www.yohana.com/)). Even Cozi's Gold tier reportedly outsells its AI tier ([36Kr](https://www.36kr.com/p/3896941067716481), snippet only), which suggests parents pay for a reliable shared calendar, not for AI.

Semli's true differentiators would carry over: a per-item "read from here" review (Skylight's model is import first, then a "reverse import" email to undo ([Skylight Zendesk](https://skylight.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/18039340445211-How-To-Use-Skylight-Magic-Import))), diffing a revised sports schedule, and ringing alarms. But they are workflow refinements inside a product category that requires three things Semli lacks. The first is **continuous inbox ingestion**: the named parent frustration is last-minute changes ([MHSAA](https://www.mhsaa.com/topics/viewpoints/parents-master-art-schedule-juggling?page=12)), which means reading email, not one-off screenshots. Semli is local-first and has no email pipeline. The second is **a multi-adult shared calendar with two-way editing**. The third is **Android coverage for the second parent**. The incumbents (Skylight, Cozi, TimeTree, FamilyWall, Nori, Fambot) are all cross-platform, and Sense, the one iPhone-only rival found, has 8 ratings ([kinmory](https://www.kinmory.ai/blog/email_to_calendar_app)). Distribution is also expensive: micro parenting creators charge $300 to $3,000 per Reel ([influencerfee.com](https://influencerfee.com/blog/parenting-influencer-cost/)), and SEO "alternative to X" pages are already saturated by funded rivals. Option (b) is a red ocean whose ceiling is real but whose entry ticket is a new product.

## Staying in college is crowded, but the working student is the overlap

The earlier competitor analysis settled the college baseline. Syllabus and screenshot import is commoditized: at least a dozen dedicated apps do it, several with review steps, at prices as low as **$5.99 a semester (ClassPulse)** ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/classpulse-gpa-study-plan/id6805395851)). Apple's iOS 26 adds screenshot-to-calendar, and Google gives US students a free year of AI Pro if they sign up by Dec 31, 2026 ([MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/08/19/students-free-year-google-ai-pro/)). Students also resist paying. MyStudyLife draws paywall anger ([AppBrain](https://www.appbrain.com/app/my-study-life-school-planner/com.virblue.mystudylife)), and Semli's own small survey put a reasonable price at $1 to $5 a month. A general college planner has no moat and a low ceiling.

The working student changes the picture because that person sits inside both markets. **40% of full-time and 74% of part-time undergraduates were employed** in 2020 ([NCES](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/coe/indicator/ssa/college-student-employment/), snippet), which is roughly 7 to 9 million people if undergraduate enrollment is 15 to 16 million. Workers under 25 are about a fifth of all hourly workers ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/opub/reports/minimum-wage/2024)), and many work at exactly the chains with no calendar export: Target, Walmart, Starbucks. Teens and young adults are also the most iPhone-heavy group in the country, at **88% iPhone ownership among teens** ([AppleInsider](https://appleinsider.com/articles/25/04/09/iphone-still-coveted-by-teens-but-other-apple-products-not-so-much)). That neutralizes the platform risk that cripples (a) and (b). No consumer app was found combining classes, shifts, deadlines and cross-type conflict flags. Nursing students are the sharpest sub-segment: clinicals are released "at the last possible second" ([allnurses](https://allnurses.com/working-nursing-school-t472710)), most also work per-diem healthcare jobs ([r/StudentNurse mirror](https://lr.eu.psf.lt/r/StudentNurse/comments/1e4vloa/what_do_you_guys_do_for_work_while_in_nursing)), and they have NurseGrid's coworker-sharing loop as precedent. But DormWay and Fhynix already market to nursing students ([DormWay](https://dormway.app/blog/best-nursing-student-planner-app-for-2026-automating-clinicals-and-deadlines)), so nursing is a contested niche. The honest weakness is that **no forum thread was found asking for an app that merges shifts and classes**. Students cope with Google Calendar and paper. The population is large, but pull is unproven.

What transfers matters most for a solo founder, and here the options diverge sharply. The codebase already contains a schedule diff (`packages/core/src/diff.ts`, which pairs shifts by date and greatest overlap into added, changed, removed and unchanged), interval and conflict logic, rescheduling, a native alarm engine (`apps/mobile/src/native/AlarmEngine.swift`), the extraction package with its evidence line, live share links with a web view (`packages/core/src/sharing.ts`), RevenueCat paywalls, and Canvas/Brightspace sync.

| Asset | (a) Hourly workers | (b) Parents | (c) Working/nursing students |
|---|---|---|---|
| Screenshot → shifts with evidence line | Direct | Partial (flyers differ from schedules; email matters more) | Direct |
| Schedule diff vs saved items | Direct, the core feature | Partial (sports revisions) | Direct |
| Cross-type conflicts | Direct (jobs, school, childcare) | Direct (kids, parents) | Direct |
| Ringing alarms | Direct | Useful | Direct |
| Canvas/Brightspace, syllabus import | Wasted | Wasted | Direct |
| Live share link / web view | Partner or childcare view | Partial stand-in for Android parent; not two-way | Share with manager or roommate |
| Local-first, single-user model | Fits | Conflicts with multi-adult editing | Fits |
| Missing pieces | Android, employer iCal ingest | Email ingestion, multi-user sync, Android | Employer iCal ingest |
| Estimated rewrite | Small to medium | Large | Small |

## Price ceilings decide this more than pain does

Put the numbers side by side and the trade-off is stark. Parents have the highest ceiling ($40 to $80 a year, higher for co-parents) but the most competition and the most new code. Hourly workers have the biggest population but a $10 to $35 ceiling and an Android-heavy base. College students have the lowest ceiling ($13 to $35) but the best platform fit and the least new code. At $20 a year, $20K MRR takes about 12,000 paying subscribers. At $60 a year it takes about 4,000. The family market would justify the work only if Semli could win those 4,000 households against Skylight, Amazon and Apple, which the evidence gives no reason to expect from an iPhone-only solo app.

| | (a) Hourly workers | (b) Parents | (c) Working/nursing students |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pain size | Very large, strongly evidenced | Large, moderately evidenced | Medium, weakly evidenced outside nursing |
| Price ceiling | ~$10–35/yr (PicCal, Supershift, Nursly) | ~$40–80/yr; co-parents $77–353 | ~$13–35/yr; survey $1–5/mo |
| Competition | 234+ shift apps; 6+ with 1M installs; AI importers new and tiny | Skylight, Amazon, Apple, Google, 8+ funded AI startups | Dozen syllabus scanners, Canvas, free AI |
| Free substitute | Employer iCal feeds; iOS screenshot import | Alexa+, Visual Intelligence, Gemini | iOS 26, Gemini, Canvas Student |
| Unclaimed edge | Diff; cross-employer conflicts | Diff of revised schedules; review step | Classes + shifts + conflicts bundle; diff |
| Platform risk (iPhone-only) | High | High (second parent) | Low |
| Distribution | Long-tail SEO ("add Starbucks schedule to calendar"), TikTok; no profession hub | Mom creators, SEO already saturated, retail for leader | Campus channels, nursing cohorts, existing listing |
| Monetization precedent | Poor (NurseGrid, Branch, Shyft went B2B) | Good (Skylight, Cozi) but via hardware/scale | Mixed (paywall backlash) |
| Code transfer | High | Low to medium | Highest |

There is a steelman for each pivot. For (a), the Fair Workweek angle is real. A timestamped change log is evidence for predictability pay in New York, Chicago, Seattle, Philadelphia, Los Angeles and Oregon, where Chicago owes 50% of pay for shifts cancelled with under 24 hours' notice ([Workforce.com](https://www.workforce.com/news/predictive-scheduling-laws)). That could turn a calendar into a money tool. But no source shows workers asking for it, and it covers a minority of workers. For (b), sports families spend $1,500 a child a year, and a revised tournament bracket is exactly what Semli's diff handles. But that wedge still needs the other parent on board, and Skylight already targets youth-sports parents ([YSBR](https://youthsportsbusinessreport.com/the-one-product-solving-the-youth-sports-parents-scheduling-crisis/)).

## The cheapest tests and the numbers that decide them

None of the three options needs new code to test, and the working-student test needs almost none, because Semli already ships the product. Run all three at the same time over six to eight weeks, with a fixed budget of roughly $300 to $600 in ads or creator posts per option.

**Working students (c-narrow) and the hourly bridge.** Use App Store custom product pages and Apple Search Ads on long-tail terms ("work schedule to calendar", "two jobs schedule", "Starbucks schedule calendar", "clinical schedule"), with screenshots showing the shift diff and a class/shift conflict. Separately, post two or three short TikTok or Reels clips of the "manager sent a new schedule photo, Semli shows what changed" moment. Then interview 15 to 20 users who import shifts, drawn from in-app prompts plus r/StudentNurse and campus job boards. Ask how they get shifts today, whether their employer offers an iCal feed, and what a missed shift has cost them. Track whether non-students install from the hourly-worker page; that is the free read on option (a). **Go** if, within eight weeks, at least 30% of new activated users import a work schedule, at least 25% of those re-import a revised schedule within 21 days (proof the diff is used), Plus trial-to-paid reaches at least 4%, and 8 of 15 interviewees describe a missed or double-booked shift in the last semester. **Kill** the working-student positioning if fewer than 15% of activated users import shifts, or if most shift users say an employer feed already solves it. In that case, add employer iCal ingestion before testing again rather than pushing screenshots.

**Hourly workers (a).** Build a one-page site off the existing web app ("Your new schedule, compared with the old one, in your calendar"), with a waitlist and an Android interest checkbox. Pair it with SEO pages for "add [employer] schedule to calendar" and $300 in Meta or TikTok ads aimed at retail and food-service workers. Charge a pre-order (for example, $19.99 for a year) instead of only collecting emails. **Go** only if the page gets 500+ visitors, converts at least 10% to the waitlist, at least 2% pay the pre-order, and fewer than half of signups ask for Android. **Kill** if paid pre-orders are under 1% or Android requests exceed 50%, because that would mean rebuilding the app for a $20-a-year customer.

**Parents (b).** A fake-door page ("Forward the revised sports schedule. See what changed. Both parents get it.") at $59 a year, promoted through two micro mom-creator posts and three youth-sports team-parent Facebook groups. Add five concierge trials in which the founder hand-processes forwarded schedules for two weeks. **Go** only if at least 3% of visitors pre-pay, at least 3 of 5 concierge families forward a second, revised schedule unprompted, and fewer than a third of households have an Android adult. **Kill** on any miss. On current evidence, expect a kill: the price ceiling is high, but the needed product is a different app.

Two cross-cutting checks are cheap and should happen first. Open the App Store by hand to confirm prices and ratings for PicCal, ShiftKal, Supershift, ClassPulse and Skylight, and search there for any shift app that diffs schedules. Then check iOS 27 for batch photo-to-Calendar import. If Apple ships batch import with change detection, every option loses its edge, and that is the most important external kill signal.

## Conclusion

The research inverts the usual pivot story. The biggest pain (hourly workers) has the weakest wallet. The best wallet (parents) is already served by a $50M-financed incumbent and three tech giants giving the feature away. The least exciting option, staying with students, turns out to be the only one where Semli's code, platform and price all fit. Its value lies less in the college market itself than in being the cheapest entry point to the hourly market. The working student is an hourly worker who happens to carry an iPhone and a class schedule. If that person adopts the shift diff and pays for it, Semli has earned the right to widen to non-student shift workers, probably adding Android and employer iCal ingestion at that point. If they don't, a full hourly pivot would have failed for the same reasons, only more expensively.

Two days after launch, the founder's real asset is not market evidence. It is a working app that can serve as its own experiment. The mistake to avoid is treating "no usage data yet" as a reason to pivot. It is a reason to point the existing product at the sharpest segment and let eight weeks of installs, re-imports and paid conversions make the decision.
